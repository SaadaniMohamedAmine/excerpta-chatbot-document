Summarize the tension between the judicial review concept in Marbury v. Madison and Hamilton's description of the judiciary in Federalist 78.

E

The tension primarily lies in the perceived power of the judiciary. Hamilton, in Federalist No. 78, characterizes the judiciary as the "least dangerous" branch, arguing it has "no influence over either the sword or the purse" Fed 78, p.2.

However, Chief Justice Marshall's ruling in Marbury v. Madison establishes the principle of judicial review, asserting that "it is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is" Marbury, p.14. This effectively gives the court the power to invalidate acts of the legislature, a power arguably stronger than Hamilton's initial characterization suggested.

"The interpretation of the laws is the proper and peculiar province of the courts. A constitution is, in fact, and must be regarded by the judges, as a fundamental law. It therefore belongs to them to ascertain its meaning, as well as the meaning of any particular act proceeding from the legislative body."

— Alexander Hamilton, Federalist No. 78
